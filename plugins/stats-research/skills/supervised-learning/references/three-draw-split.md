# The three-draw split: why it works and where it fails

In a simulation, the `supervised-learning` skill gives each dataset three independent draws, each with the $n$ observations of the real-data analysis it stands for, so $3n$ observations in all. The learners are fit on the training draw, tuned and chosen on the validation draw, and the estimators run on the estimation draw. This file calls that design the three-draw split. It gives the argument that the three-draw split emulates cross-validated cross-fitting at sample size $n$, which the skill calls the nested scheme, works through the cases where it does not, and records the checks behind the numbers in the skill.

## Notation

- $O = (X, A, Y)$ is one observation, with covariates $X$, a binary treatment $A$ and an outcome $Y$.
- $E$ is an expectation under the data distribution $P$, $\Pr$ is a probability under it, and $Pf = E[f(O)]$ is the mean of a function $f$ at a new observation. When $f$ contains a fit, the fit is held fixed. For example, $P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta)$ below is the mean of the AIPW score $\phi(O; \hat\eta)$ over a new observation $O$ with the fit $\hat\eta$ held fixed, so it is random only through $\hat\eta$.
- $\pi_0(X) = \Pr(A = 1 \mid X)$ is the true propensity score, and $\mu_0(a, X) = E[Y \mid A = a, X]$ is the true outcome regression. Together they are the true nuisance functions $\eta_0 = (\pi_0, \mu_0)$. Without the subscript 0, $\pi$, $\mu$ and $\eta = (\pi, \mu)$ denote any candidate functions of the same kind. A hat marks an estimate: a fitted function such as $\hat\eta$, a fitted parameter, or an estimator. The subscript 0 always marks the truth, never a parameter value of zero.
- $\psi_0 = E[\mu_0(1, X) - \mu_0(0, X)]$ is the true average treatment effect.
- For any candidate $\eta$, the augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) score is
$$\phi(O; \eta) = \mu(1, X) - \mu(0, X) + \frac{A}{\pi(X)}\{Y - \mu(1, X)\} - \frac{1 - A}{1 - \pi(X)}\{Y - \mu(0, X)\}.$$
  The AIPW estimate is the average of the AIPW scores at the fitted nuisance functions, $\phi(O_i; \hat\eta)$, over the observations it is computed on. The efficient influence function of the average treatment effect $\psi_0$ is $\phi(O; \eta_0) - \psi_0$, the centred AIPW score at the truth.
- For a set of observations, $\mathbb{P}$ with that set as subscript is the average over it, and $(\mathbb{P} - P)f$ means $\mathbb{P}f - Pf$. The sets are the training draw $\mathcal{T}$, the validation draw $\mathcal{V}$ and the estimation draw $\mathcal{E}$ of the three-draw split, the cross-fitting folds below, and the data $\mathcal{F}$ that a fit was trained on. $\mathbb{P}_n$ averages over a full sample of $n$ observations.
- For a function $f$ of $X$, $\|f\|^2 = E[f(X)^2]$, again with any fit held fixed. For the outcome regression, $\|\mu - \mu_0\| = \|\mu(1, \cdot) - \mu_0(1, \cdot)\| + \|\mu(0, \cdot) - \mu_0(0, \cdot)\|$ adds this norm over the two arms. When the propensity score is bounded away from 0 and 1, it is equivalent to the $L_2(P)$ norm of $\mu(A, X) - \mu_0(A, X)$.
- $K$ is the number of cross-fitting folds, fixed as $n$ grows, and $V$ is the number of folds in the cross-validation that runs inside each training set.
- $\approx$ means equal up to terms of smaller order than those kept, which is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$ in an expansion of an estimate. The variance of an estimator below means the variance of the leading average in its expansion, that is, its asymptotic variance divided by $n$. Between such variances, $\approx$ means equal up to $o(1/n)$, and between $n$ times them, up to $o(1)$. $o_P$ and $O_P$ are the usual stochastic order symbols.

## What the three-draw split stands in for

In $K$-fold cross-fitting, a sample of $n$ observations is divided into folds $I_1, \dots, I_K$ of $n/K$ observations each. The out-of-fold fit $\hat\eta_{-k}$ is trained on every fold except $I_k$, which is $(K-1)n/K$ observations, and it predicts $I_k$. The cross-fit estimate is
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{cf}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i \in I_k} \phi(O_i; \hat\eta_{-k}) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{P}_{I_k}\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{-k}).$$
Every observation sits in exactly one fold, so the estimate averages over all $n$ observations. When the learners are tuned, each training set holds a $V$-fold cross-validation, and every setting is then refit on the whole training set. That is $V + 1$ fits per setting in each of $K$ training sets, $K(V+1)$ per setting in all. The skill calls this tuned version the nested scheme. With nothing to tune, as in the checks at the end of this file, the nested scheme is plain cross-fitting.

The three-draw split fits $\hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}$ on the training draw $\mathcal{T}$, tunes it on the validation draw $\mathcal{V}$, and computes
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} = \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}}\,\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}})$$
on the estimation draw $\mathcal{E}$. The three draws are independent, and each setting is fit once.

## The first-order argument

**The conditions.** The argument uses four conditions, for some constants $c > 0$ and $M$.

- *Overlap:* the true propensity score and every fitted propensity lie in $[c, 1 - c]$.
- *Bounded noise:* $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid A, X) \le M$.
- *Consistency:* the fits' errors $\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\|$ and $\|\hat\mu - \mu_0\|$ tend to zero in probability.
- *Rate:* the product $\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\|\,\|\hat\mu - \mu_0\|$ is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$.

**Decompose the error.** Subtract the true effect $\psi_0$, and add and subtract $P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}})$, the population mean of the AIPW score at the split's fit:
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0 = (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) + \{P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \psi_0\}.$$
Then add and subtract $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)$, the same centred average at the truth, to split the first term:
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0 = \underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)}_{\text{leading term}} + \underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\{\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)\}}_{\text{empirical-process term}} + \underbrace{P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \psi_0}_{\text{remainder}}.$$

**The remainder is the mean of a product of errors.** Fix a candidate $\eta$ and take expectations in three steps. First condition on $X$. Given $(A, X)$, the product $A\{Y - \mu(1, X)\}$ is zero unless $A = 1$, which has probability $\pi_0(X)$ given $X$, and then $Y$ has mean $\mu_0(1, X)$. So $E[A\{Y - \mu(1, X)\} \mid X] = \pi_0(X)\{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\}$, and in the same way $E[(1 - A)\{Y - \mu(0, X)\} \mid X] = \{1 - \pi_0(X)\}\{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\}$. The weights $1/\pi(X)$ and $1/\{1 - \pi(X)\}$ are fixed given $X$, so
$$E[\phi(O; \eta) \mid X] = \mu(1, X) - \mu(0, X) + \frac{\pi_0(X)}{\pi(X)}\{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\} - \frac{1 - \pi_0(X)}{1 - \pi(X)}\{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\}.$$
Next subtract the true conditional effect $\mu_0(1, X) - \mu_0(0, X)$, whose mean over $X$ is the true effect $\psi_0$. The first two terms then become $-\{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\} + \{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\}$, so
$$E[\phi(O; \eta) \mid X] - \{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu_0(0, X)\} = \frac{\pi_0(X)}{\pi(X)}\{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\} - \{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\} - \frac{1 - \pi_0(X)}{1 - \pi(X)}\{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\} + \{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\}.$$
Collecting the two terms of each arm gives
$$E[\phi(O; \eta) \mid X] - \{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu_0(0, X)\} = \left\{\frac{\pi_0(X)}{\pi(X)} - 1\right\}\{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)\} - \left\{\frac{1 - \pi_0(X)}{1 - \pi(X)} - 1\right\}\{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)\}.$$
Finally use $\pi_0/\pi - 1 = (\pi_0 - \pi)/\pi$ and $(1 - \pi_0)/(1 - \pi) - 1 = -(\pi_0 - \pi)/(1 - \pi)$. In the control arm, the second minus sign cancels the one in front of the bracket, which is why the result has a plus. Factor out the propensity error $\pi_0 - \pi$ and average over $X$, which turns the left side into $P\phi(\cdot\,; \eta) - \psi_0$ by the tower property:
$$P\phi(\cdot\,; \eta) - \psi_0 = E\left[\{\pi_0(X) - \pi(X)\}\left\{\frac{\mu_0(1, X) - \mu(1, X)}{\pi(X)} + \frac{\mu_0(0, X) - \mu(0, X)}{1 - \pi(X)}\right\}\right].$$
Overlap keeps every term finite. The remainder is zero whenever the candidate propensity is the true one, $\pi = \pi_0$, whatever the candidate outcome regression $\mu$ is, and whenever $\mu = \mu_0$, whatever $\pi$ is. This is double robustness. The next section uses its first half, and that section's mirror case uses the second. Setting the candidate to the truth, $\eta = \eta_0$, gives $P\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0) = \psi_0$: the AIPW score at the truth has mean equal to the true effect.

**The remainder is small when both fits are good.** By overlap, $1/\hat\pi$ and $1/(1 - \hat\pi)$ are at most $1/c$. Bounding $|E[Z]|$ by $E|Z|$, then applying the triangle inequality, and then the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in each arm, gives
$$|P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta) - \psi_0| \le \frac{1}{c}\, E\big[|\hat\pi(X) - \pi_0(X)|\,\{|\hat\mu(1, X) - \mu_0(1, X)| + |\hat\mu(0, X) - \mu_0(0, X)|\}\big] \le \frac{1}{c}\,\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\|\,\|\hat\mu - \mu_0\|.$$
So by the rate condition the remainder is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$.

**The leading term is an average of independent terms.** It averages, over the estimation draw, $n$ independent copies of the efficient influence function $\phi(O; \eta_0) - \psi_0$, which have mean zero because $P\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0) = \psi_0$. Its variance is $\operatorname{Var}\phi(O; \eta_0)/n$.

**The empirical-process term is small because the estimation draw is fresh.** The split's fit depends on the training draw and, through tuning, on the validation draw, but not on the estimation draw. Given both, write $\Delta(O) = \phi(O; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \phi(O; \eta_0)$ for the difference of scores, which is then a fixed function. The empirical-process term averages, over the estimation draw, $n$ independent copies of $\Delta(O) - P\Delta$, so its conditional variance is $\operatorname{Var}(\Delta \mid \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V})/n$. To bound it, add and subtract $\mu_0(a, X)$ in each residual of the fitted score:
$$\Delta = \Big(1 - \frac{A}{\hat\pi}\Big)\{\hat\mu(1, X) - \mu_0(1, X)\} - \Big(1 - \frac{1 - A}{1 - \hat\pi}\Big)\{\hat\mu(0, X) - \mu_0(0, X)\} + (\pi_0 - \hat\pi)\left[\frac{A\{Y - \mu_0(1, X)\}}{\hat\pi\,\pi_0} + \frac{(1 - A)\{Y - \mu_0(0, X)\}}{(1 - \hat\pi)(1 - \pi_0)}\right].$$
By overlap, the weights in the first two terms are at most $1/c$ in absolute value, and the denominators in the bracket are at least $c^2$. By bounded noise, $E[A\{Y - \mu_0(1, X)\}^2 \mid X] \le M$, and likewise in the control arm. Squaring with $(a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + a_4)^2 \le 4(a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 + a_4^2)$ then gives
$$\operatorname{Var}(\Delta \mid \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V}) \le E[\Delta^2 \mid \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V}] \le 4\left\{\frac{\|\hat\mu - \mu_0\|^2}{c^2} + \frac{2M\,\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\|^2}{c^4}\right\},$$
which tends to zero in probability by consistency. By Chebyshev's inequality given $(\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V})$, for any $t > 0$,
$$\Pr\big(\sqrt{n}\,|\text{empirical-process term}| > t \,\big|\, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V}\big) \le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta \mid \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V})}{t^2}.$$
By the tower property, the unconditional probability is the mean of this conditional probability, which is at most the smaller of 1 and the right-hand side. A quantity bounded by 1 that tends to zero in probability has a mean that tends to zero. So $\Pr(\sqrt{n}\,|\text{empirical-process term}| > t) \to 0$ for every $t > 0$, which is the definition of the term being $o_P(n^{-1/2})$. No condition on the complexity of the learner is needed. That is the reason for keeping the fitting data apart from the estimation data.

**The split's error.** Under the four conditions, the split's error $\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0$ equals the leading term $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)$ plus $o_P(n^{-1/2})$.

**Cross-fitting.** Start from $\hat\psi_{\mathrm{cf}} - \psi_0 = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k \{\mathbb{P}_{I_k}\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{-k}) - \psi_0\}$, and decompose each fold's term as above, with the fold $I_k$ in place of the estimation draw and the out-of-fold fit $\hat\eta_{-k}$ in place of the split's fit. Fold $I_k$ is independent of the data that trained $\hat\eta_{-k}$, so the same bounds apply, with two changes that do not matter for fixed $K$. Each fold averages $n/K$ observations, which multiplies the variance of its empirical-process term by $K$, and each fit trains on $(K-1)n/K$ observations. Averaging over the folds, the leading terms give $\frac{1}{K}\sum_k (\mathbb{P}_{I_k} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0) = (\mathbb{P}_n - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)$, and the other terms, $K$ of each kind, are again $o_P(n^{-1/2})$ (Chernozhukov et al., 2018).

So both estimators equal the true effect $\psi_0$, plus an average of the efficient influence function over $n$ independent observations, plus terms that shrink faster than $1/\sqrt{n}$. Their sampling distributions agree to first order. Training on $n$ observations instead of the $(K-1)n/K$ in each cross-fitting training set changes only the smaller terms, through the size of the fits' errors.

**The reported standard errors agree too.** In both schemes, the reported standard error is the standard deviation of the fitted AIPW scores $\phi(O_i; \hat\eta)$, pooled over folds under cross-fitting, divided by $\sqrt{n}$. For the split, the triangle inequality puts the empirical standard deviations of $\phi(O_i; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}})$ and of $\phi(O_i; \eta_0)$ over the estimation draw within $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}}\Delta^2)^{1/2}$ of each other. Given $(\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V})$, the average $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}}\Delta^2$ has mean $E[\Delta^2 \mid \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{V}]$, which tends to zero in probability by the bound above, so the average does too, by Markov's inequality and the same conditioning step. The empirical standard deviation of $\phi(O_i; \eta_0)$ converges to that of $\phi(O; \eta_0)$ by the law of large numbers. Under cross-fitting the same holds fold by fold. So in both schemes the reported standard error estimates $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\phi(O; \eta_0)/n}$, the standard deviation of the leading term, and the intervals agree as well.

**What the argument needs.** It needs an orthogonal estimator, meaning one whose remainder is second order in the fits' errors, as it is for AIPW, targeted maximum likelihood estimation and double machine learning. It also needs the consistency and rate conditions, which say that the fits' errors are small at the sample size in use. The next two sections relax these. The first takes a fit that converges to the wrong function. A non-orthogonal estimator such as inverse probability weighting falls under it, since it is AIPW with the outcome regression set to zero. The second takes fits whose errors are still large at the sample size in use.

## When a nuisance fit converges to the wrong function

**Setup.** Suppose the outcome fit converges, in the norm above, to some limit $\bar\mu$ other than the true outcome regression $\mu_0$, with $\mu_0 - \bar\mu$ bounded. Suppose the propensity fit is $\hat\pi = \pi_{\hat\theta}$, the maximum-likelihood fit of a correctly specified parametric model $\pi_\theta$, whose true parameter $\theta_0$ gives $\pi_{\theta_0} = \pi_0$. Assume that $\pi_\theta$ is twice continuously differentiable in $\theta$ and lies in $[c, 1 - c]$ for $\theta$ near $\theta_0$. Write $p_\theta(A \mid X) = \pi_\theta(X)^A\{1 - \pi_\theta(X)\}^{1 - A}$ for the model's density of the treatment, $S(O) = \partial_\theta \log p_\theta(A \mid X)\,|_{\theta_0}$ for its likelihood score, and $J = E[SS^\top]$ for its information. Write $\bar\eta = (\pi_0, \bar\mu)$ for the limit of the two fits, $\bar\phi(O) = \phi(O; \bar\eta)$ for the AIPW score at that limit, and $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}\bar\phi(O)$ for its variance.

**Re-centre the decomposition at the limit.** Adding and subtracting $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\bar\phi$ in place of $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)$ gives the same decomposition around the limit $\bar\eta$:
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0 = (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\bar\phi + (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\{\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \bar\phi\} + \{P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \psi_0\}.$$
This identity holds for any $\bar\eta$. Around the truth, the empirical-process term would contain $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\{\bar\phi - \phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0)\}$, which is of order $n^{-1/2}$. Around the limit, the middle term is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$ by the same conditional-variance argument, with the fits converging to $\bar\eta$. That argument needs one change: the propensity fit's error now multiplies $Y - \bar\mu(a, X)$, whose conditional second moment is $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid A, X) + \{\mu_0(a, X) - \bar\mu(a, X)\}^2$, and this is bounded because $\mu_0 - \bar\mu$ is. Double robustness with $\pi = \pi_0$ gives $P\bar\phi = \psi_0$, so the limit of the fits creates no first-order bias. The first term has variance $\sigma^2/n$, where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of the limiting AIPW score $\bar\phi$.

**The propensity fit's error now enters the remainder at first order.** Split the outcome fit's error in the remainder formula as $\mu_0 - \hat\mu = (\mu_0 - \bar\mu) + (\bar\mu - \hat\mu)$:
$$P\phi(\cdot\,; \hat\eta_{\mathcal{T}}) - \psi_0 = \underbrace{E\left[(\pi_0 - \hat\pi)\left\{\frac{\mu_0(1, X) - \bar\mu(1, X)}{\hat\pi} + \frac{\mu_0(0, X) - \bar\mu(0, X)}{1 - \hat\pi}\right\}\right]}_{=\,P\phi(\cdot\,;\, \hat\pi, \bar\mu) - \psi_0} + \underbrace{E\left[(\pi_0 - \hat\pi)\left\{\frac{\bar\mu(1, X) - \hat\mu(1, X)}{\hat\pi} + \frac{\bar\mu(0, X) - \hat\mu(0, X)}{1 - \hat\pi}\right\}\right]}_{\le\, c^{-1}\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\|\,\|\hat\mu - \bar\mu\|}.$$
The second part is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$, because $\|\hat\pi - \pi_0\| = O_P(n^{-1/2})$ for a parametric fit and $\|\hat\mu - \bar\mu\| \to 0$. In the first part, the second factor tends to
$$b(X) = \frac{\mu_0(1, X) - \bar\mu(1, X)}{\pi_0(X)} + \frac{\mu_0(0, X) - \bar\mu(0, X)}{1 - \pi_0(X)}.$$
A direct calculation from the definition of the AIPW score gives $\bar\phi - \phi(\cdot\,; \eta_0) = \{A - \pi_0(X)\}\,b(X)$, so $b$ is nonzero unless the limiting score equals the score at the truth. When $b$ is nonzero, the first part is, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, at most of the order of the propensity fit's error, $O_P(n^{-1/2})$, and in general it does not vanish. Expanding it in the propensity model's parameter $\theta$, with the outcome regression held at $\bar\mu$,
$$P\phi(\cdot\,; \pi_{\hat\theta}, \bar\mu) - \psi_0 = \underbrace{\{P\phi(\cdot\,; \pi_{\theta_0}, \bar\mu) - \psi_0\}}_{=\,0} + D^\top(\hat\theta - \theta_0) + O_P(1/n), \qquad D = \partial_\theta\, P\phi(\cdot\,; \pi_\theta, \bar\mu)\big|_{\theta_0}.$$
The first term is zero by double robustness, and the last is the quadratic term of the expansion, which is $O_P(1/n)$ because $\hat\theta - \theta_0 = O_P(n^{-1/2})$.

**The likelihood score fixes $D$.** Consider the data distribution $P_\theta$ in which the treatment follows the parametric model $\pi_\theta$ and everything else is unchanged, and write $E_\theta$ for expectations under it. Its average treatment effect is still $\psi_0$, because $\psi_0$ depends only on the distribution of $X$ and on that of $Y$ given $(A, X)$. By double robustness under $P_\theta$, AIPW with the correct propensity $\pi_\theta$ has mean $\psi_0$ whatever the outcome regression. So $E_\theta[\phi(O; \pi_\theta, \bar\mu)] = \psi_0$ for every $\theta$. Differentiate at the true parameter $\theta_0$. Write $p(y \mid a, x)$ and $p(x)$ for the densities of the outcome given treatment and covariates and of the covariates, which do not depend on $\theta$, Passing the derivative under the integral, it falls either on the AIPW score or on the density $p_\theta$, and $\partial_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta\,\partial_\theta \log p_\theta$. At $\theta_0$, the densities combine into $dP$ and the AIPW score becomes $\bar\phi$, since $\pi_{\theta_0} = \pi_0$. So
$$0 = \partial_\theta \sum_{a = 0}^{1} \iint \phi\{(x, a, y); \pi_\theta, \bar\mu\}\, p_\theta(a \mid x)\, p(y \mid a, x)\, p(x)\, dy\, dx\,\Big|_{\theta_0} = E\big[\partial_\theta\phi(O; \pi_\theta, \bar\mu)\big|_{\theta_0}\big] + E[\bar\phi(O)\, S(O)].$$
The first expectation is the derivative $D$, because $P$ itself does not depend on $\theta$, and the second is the mean product of the limiting AIPW score $\bar\phi$ and the likelihood score $S$. So $D = -E[\bar\phi\, S]$. Differentiating $\sum_a p_\theta(a \mid x) = 1$ gives $E[S] = 0$, so the derivative is minus the covariance of the limiting AIPW score with the likelihood score.

**The maximum-likelihood expansion turns the remainder into an average.** Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the data the propensity model was fit on. Write $\ell_\theta(O) = \log p_\theta(A \mid X)$, and $\dot\ell_\theta$ and $\ddot\ell_\theta$ for its first and second derivatives in $\theta$, so that $S = \dot\ell_{\theta_0}$. The fitted parameter solves $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\dot\ell_{\hat\theta} = 0$. A Taylor expansion around $\theta_0$ gives
$$0 = \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}} S + (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\ddot\ell_{\tilde\theta})(\hat\theta - \theta_0),$$
with each row of the second derivative evaluated at some $\tilde\theta$ between $\hat\theta$ and $\theta_0$. By consistency and the law of large numbers, $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\ddot\ell_{\tilde\theta}$ tends in probability to $E[\ddot\ell_{\theta_0}]$, which is $-J$ by the information identity. Since $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}S = O_P(n^{-1/2})$, replacing the random matrix by $-J$ costs $o_P(n^{-1/2})$, so $\hat\theta - \theta_0 \approx J^{-1}\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}} S$, the inverse information times the average likelihood score over the fitting data. Then
$$P\phi(\cdot\,; \pi_{\hat\theta}, \bar\mu) - \psi_0 \approx D^\top(\hat\theta - \theta_0) \approx -E[\bar\phi\, S^\top]\, J^{-1}\,\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}} S = -\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\, h, \qquad h(O) = E[\bar\phi\, S^\top]\, J^{-1} S(O).$$

**$h$ is the regression of the limiting AIPW score on the likelihood score.** The population least-squares coefficient of $\bar\phi$ on $S$ is $E[SS^\top]^{-1}E[S\bar\phi] = J^{-1}E[S\bar\phi]$. Because $E[S] = 0$, this slope is the same with or without an intercept. Transposing the scalar $h(O)$ and using $J = J^\top$ gives $h(O) = S(O)^\top J^{-1}E[S\bar\phi]$, the fitted value of the regression through the origin. Three facts follow.

- $E[h] = E[\bar\phi\, S^\top]\, J^{-1} E[S] = 0$.
- $\operatorname{Cov}(\bar\phi, h) = E[\bar\phi\, h]$, since $E[h] = 0$, and this equals $E[\bar\phi\, S^\top]\, J^{-1} E[S\bar\phi]$. $\operatorname{Var} h = E[\bar\phi\, S^\top]\, J^{-1} J\, J^{-1} E[S\bar\phi]$ is the same quantity.
- Hence the residual $\bar\phi - h$ is uncorrelated with the fit $h$, and the limiting score's variance splits as $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var} h + \operatorname{Var}(\bar\phi - h)$.

Write $r = \operatorname{Var} h/\sigma^2$ for the share of the limiting score's variance that the likelihood score explains. By the last fact, $r$ lies between 0 and 1. It is the population $R^2$ of the regression.

**The variance under each scheme.**

*True propensity score.* There is no propensity fit, and the remainder is exactly zero by double robustness. The estimate is therefore the true effect $\psi_0$ plus the centred average of the limiting AIPW score, $(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\bar\phi$, up to $o_P(n^{-1/2})$, with variance $\sigma^2/n$. Cross-fitting with the true propensity score has the same variance, with the full-sample average $\mathbb{P}_n$ in place of the estimation-draw average $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}}$.

*Three-draw split.* The propensity model is fit on the training draw, so $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{T}$, and
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0 \approx (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\bar\phi - \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\, h.$$
The two terms average over different draws, so they are independent. Their variances are $\sigma^2/n$ and $\operatorname{Var} h/n = r\sigma^2/n$, where $r$ is the explained share defined above, so $\operatorname{Var}\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} \approx (1 + r)\,\sigma^2/n$.

*Cross-fitting.* Fold $k$'s propensity fit is trained on the other $K - 1$ folds, written $-k$, so $\mathbb{P}_{-k}h = \frac{K}{(K-1)n}\sum_{i \notin I_k} h(O_i)$. Each observation lies outside exactly $K - 1$ of the $K$ folds, so averaging over folds gives
$$\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{P}_{-k}\, h = \frac{1}{K} \cdot \frac{K}{(K-1)\,n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i \notin I_k} h(O_i) = \frac{1}{(K-1)\,n} \cdot (K - 1)\sum_{i=1}^{n} h(O_i) = \mathbb{P}_n h.$$
The fits' errors therefore come from the same observations as the leading term. Using that the regression fit $h$ has mean zero,
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{cf}} - \psi_0 \approx (\mathbb{P}_n - P)\,\bar\phi - \mathbb{P}_n h = (\mathbb{P}_n - P)(\bar\phi - h).$$
This averages $n$ independent terms, so its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(\bar\phi - h)/n$, and by the last of the three facts, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar\phi - h) = \sigma^2 - \operatorname{Var} h = (1 - r)\,\sigma^2$.

*One fit to the full sample.* Assume here that the outcome fit is also parametric, so that both fits are functions of a finite-dimensional parameter $\gamma$, with estimate $\hat\gamma$ and limit $\bar\gamma$. Write $\phi_\gamma$ for the AIPW score at parameter $\gamma$ and $\dot\phi_\gamma$ for its gradient in $\gamma$. The same algebra applies with the propensity model's fitting data $\mathcal{F}$ equal to the full sample, so the variance is again $(1 - r)\,\sigma^2/n$. The empirical-process term needs a different argument here, because the fits saw the observations they are averaged over. A Taylor expansion in $\gamma$ gives
$$(\mathbb{P}_n - P)\{\phi_{\hat\gamma} - \phi_{\bar\gamma}\} = \{(\mathbb{P}_n - P)\,\dot\phi_{\bar\gamma}\}^\top(\hat\gamma - \bar\gamma) + (\text{second-order term}).$$
The first part is $O_P(n^{-1/2}) \cdot O_P(n^{-1/2})$, and the second is smaller still when the second derivatives of $\phi_\gamma$ have an integrable bound near $\bar\gamma$. So the term is $o_P(n^{-1/2})$.

**What the reported standard error misses.** The reported standard error is the spread of the fitted AIPW scores, which converge to $\bar\phi$. So it estimates $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, the standard deviation of the first term alone, in all four cases above. It has no term for the propensity fit's contribution $-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\, h$. The reported standard error divided by the actual one is 1 with the true propensity score, $1/\sqrt{1 - r}$ under cross-fitting or a fit to the full sample, and $1/\sqrt{1 + r}$ under the three-draw split, where $r$ is the share of $\sigma^2$ that the likelihood score explains. So cross-fitting's intervals are too wide and the split's are too narrow. The ratio of the estimates' actual standard errors, split over cross-fitting, is $\sqrt{(1 + r)/(1 - r)}$.

**Inverse probability weighting.** Inverse probability weighting is AIPW with the outcome regression set to $\mu \equiv 0$, which is a wrong limit unless the true outcome regression is zero everywhere. The cross-fitting line is then the classical result that an estimated propensity score beats the true one. The three-draw split reverses it: a propensity score estimated on an independent draw is worse than the true one.

**The mirror case: a wrong propensity model and a correct outcome model.** Suppose instead that the propensity fit converges to some limit $\bar\pi$ other than $\pi_0$, with $\hat\pi$ and $\bar\pi$ in $[c, 1 - c]$. Suppose the outcome fit $\mu_{\hat\beta}$ comes from a correctly specified parametric model $\mu_\beta$ with parameter $\beta$, whose true value $\beta_0$ gives $\mu_{\beta_0} = \mu_0$. Suppose also that the parameter error is approximately an average over the fitting data, $\hat\beta - \beta_0 \approx \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\, u$, for some mean-zero function $u$. The AIPW score at the limit is now $\tilde\phi = \phi(\cdot\,; \bar\pi, \mu_0)$, written with a tilde to keep it apart from $\bar\phi$ above, and it has mean equal to the true effect, $P\tilde\phi = \psi_0$, by the second half of double robustness. In the remainder formula, the outcome fit's error $\mu_0 - \mu_{\hat\beta}$ is now $O_P(n^{-1/2})$. Replacing $\hat\pi$ by $\bar\pi$ in the first factor and in the weights changes each weighted term by at most $c^{-2}|\hat\pi - \bar\pi|$ times that error, since for instance $|(\pi_0 - \hat\pi)/\hat\pi - (\pi_0 - \bar\pi)/\bar\pi| \le c^{-2}|\hat\pi - \bar\pi|$, so the swap costs $o_P(n^{-1/2})$. Expanding what is left in $\beta$ makes the remainder approximately $\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\, g$, with $g = G^\top u$ and
$$G = -E\left[\{\pi_0(X) - \bar\pi(X)\}\left\{\frac{\partial_\beta\mu_\beta(1, X)}{\bar\pi(X)} + \frac{\partial_\beta\mu_\beta(0, X)}{1 - \bar\pi(X)}\right\}\right]_{\beta = \beta_0},$$
where the leading minus sign comes from $\partial_\beta(\mu_0 - \mu_\beta) = -\partial_\beta\mu_\beta$. Since $E[g] = 0$, the two schemes give
$$\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} - \psi_0 \approx (\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{E}} - P)\,\tilde\phi + \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\, g, \qquad \hat\psi_{\mathrm{cf}} - \psi_0 \approx (\mathbb{P}_n - P)(\tilde\phi + g).$$
So, for the limiting score $\tilde\phi$ and the outcome fit's contribution $g$, $n\operatorname{Var}\hat\psi_{\mathrm{split}} \approx \operatorname{Var}\tilde\phi + \operatorname{Var} g$ and $n\operatorname{Var}\hat\psi_{\mathrm{cf}} \approx \operatorname{Var}\tilde\phi + \operatorname{Var} g + 2\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde\phi, g)$. In the case above, the likelihood-score argument tied the covariance to $\operatorname{Var} h$, because moving $\theta$ leaves the average treatment effect unchanged. Moving $\beta$ changes the effect, since the outcome regression determines it, so no such identity holds here, and $u$ comes from least squares rather than from a likelihood score. Nothing ties $\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde\phi, g)$ to $\operatorname{Var} g$.

In the parametric check below, the covariance is exactly zero asymptotically. There the covariate $X$ is symmetric about 0 and $\mathrm{expit}(-x) = 1 - \mathrm{expit}(x)$, so the constant propensity fit converges to $\bar\pi = E[\pi_0(X)] = 1/2$. With $\varepsilon$ the check's outcome noise, $\tilde\phi = 1 + 2(2A - 1)\varepsilon$. The outcome fit is least squares on $Z = (1, A, X)$, so $u = E[ZZ^\top]^{-1}Z\varepsilon$. By the same symmetry, the first two entries of $G$ are zero, and its last entry is $-4E[X\pi_0(X)] \approx -0.318$. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem, the last entry of $u$ is $\{X - E[X \mid A]\}\varepsilon/E[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid A)]$, so $g$ is proportional to $\{X - E[X \mid A]\}\varepsilon$. Conditioning on $(A, X)$ removes the constant 1 in $\tilde\phi$ and replaces $\varepsilon^2$ by 1, so $\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde\phi, g)$ is proportional to $E[(2A - 1)\{X - E[X \mid A]\}]$, which is zero after conditioning on $A$. With $\operatorname{Var}\tilde\phi = 4$ and $\operatorname{Var} g \approx 0.328$, the predicted empirical standard error times $\sqrt{n}$ is $\sqrt{4.328} \approx 2.08$ under every scheme, against about 2.11 observed. The reported standard error estimates $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\tilde\phi} = 2$.

**Both fits wrong.** When both fits converge to wrong functions, the estimator can be asymptotically biased, and both fits' errors enter at first order. None of the formulas above apply. The misspecified linear fits in the boosting check below fall in this case.

## When a learner is noisy

A noisy learner is one whose fits change a lot from one training sample to the next, so its errors are not small at the sample size in use. Two effects then separate the schemes. Both are tendencies that the boosting check below confirms, not theorems.

**Smaller training sets.** Each cross-fitting fit trains on $(K-1)n/K$ observations. For a noisy learner, that makes the AIPW scores at the out-of-fold fits, $\phi(O_i; \hat\eta_{-k})$, more variable. It raises both the reported standard error and the spread of a single fit's estimates. The boosting check shows this by giving the three-draw split a training draw of 400 observations, the $(K-1)n/K$ of 5-fold cross-fitting at $n = 500$, in place of 500. The reported standard error rises from 4.90 to 5.47 and the spread of the estimates from 5.07 to 6.13, all times $\sqrt{n}$. The split with 400 then reports about the same standard error as 5-fold cross-fitting, 5.47 against 5.42.

**Averaging over fits.** The cross-fit estimate averages $K$ fits whose errors are partly independent, which lowers the spread of the estimates below that of any single fit. With the training draw matched at 400, the split's estimates spread about 25% more than cross-fitting's: 6.13 against 4.91.

Together, the two effects leave cross-fitting's reported standard error above the actual spread of its estimates. The reported standard error is set by fits on $(K-1)n/K$ observations, and the averaging lowers the actual spread, so cross-fitting's intervals are conservative. Under the three-draw split, the reported and the actual standard error both come from one fit on $n$ observations, and the intervals are close to nominal. Neither effect appears in the first-order argument, which assumes that the fits' errors are small.

## The checks

All checks estimate the average treatment effect with AIPW, clip the fitted propensity to $[0.01, 0.99]$, and use 95% Wald intervals, the estimate plus or minus 1.96 reported standard errors. Each row is one scheme at $n = 500$. SE is short for standard error. The empirical SE is the standard deviation of the estimates across repetitions, the reported SE is the one the estimator reports in a single dataset, and the model-based SE is the root mean of the squared reported SEs across repetitions. The tables give both multiplied by $\sqrt{n}$. The full-sample scheme fits and estimates on the same 500 observations. Cross-fitting uses $K = 5$ unless a row says otherwise. The learners are fixed, with no tuning, so the validation draw plays no part, and cross-fitting here is the nested scheme with nothing to tune. The checks therefore test the training and estimation roles only.

**The parametric check.** $X \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-1, 1)$, the true propensity score satisfies $\mathrm{logit}\,\pi_0(X) = X$, and $Y = A + 2X + \varepsilon$ with noise $\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1)$, so the true average treatment effect is $\psi_0 = 1$. The propensity fit is a logistic regression on $X$ (correct) or a constant (wrong). The outcome fit is a linear regression on $A$ and $X$ (correct), a linear regression on $A$ alone (wrong, since it leaves out the confounder $X$), or zero (inverse probability weighting). There are 4000 repetitions. The Monte Carlo SE of a coverage is about 0.003 near 95% and 0.005 near 87%, and that of an empirical SE is about 1.1% of its value. The predicted column is the empirical SE times $\sqrt{n}$ that the formulas above predict. It uses the limiting score's standard deviation $\sigma$ and the explained share $r$, or for the constant propensity the mirror case's $\operatorname{Var}\tilde\phi$ and $\operatorname{Var} g$, all computed from the data-generating process by numerical integration.

For inverse probability weighting it gives $\sigma = 3.199$ and $r = 0.573$. The model-based SEs match these standard deviations, as the reported SE should. The ratio of empirical SEs, split over cross-fitting, is 2.08 against a predicted 2.06 for the wrong outcome fit, and 1.90 against 1.92 for inverse probability weighting. The observed empirical SEs run 1 to 4% above the predictions in every row, including the correctly specified ones, which points to a finite-sample effect at $n = 500$.

For coverage, treat the reported SE $\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{rep}}$ as a constant, and write $\hat\psi - \psi_0 = \mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{emp}} Z$, where $\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{emp}}$ is the empirical SE and $Z$ is standard normal, which holds approximately when the estimate is normal with no bias. The interval covers when $|\hat\psi - \psi_0| \le 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{rep}}$, that is, when $|Z| \le 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{rep}}/\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{emp}}$, which has probability $2\Phi(1.96\,\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{rep}}/\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{emp}}) - 1$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. With the wrong outcome fit, the ratio $\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{rep}}/\mathrm{SE}_{\mathrm{emp}}$ is $1/\sqrt{1 - r}$ under cross-fitting and $1/\sqrt{1 + r}$ under the split, from the variances above, where $r$ is the explained share. $\Phi$ is the only normal function in this file; the lowercase $\phi$ is always the AIPW score. With $r = 0.620$, the predicted coverage is 99.85% under cross-fitting and 87.6% under the split, against the observed 99.8% and 87.4%.

**The boosting check.** This uses the complex data-generating process of the `design-and-report-simulations` scaffolds. The covariates are $X = (X_1, X_2)$, with $X_1$ and $X_2$ independent and $\mathrm{Uniform}(-1, 1)$, written `W1` and `W2` in the scaffold code. The true propensity score has $\mathrm{logit}\,\pi_0(X) = X_1 + X_2 + X_1|X_2|$. The true outcome regression is $\mu_0(a, X) = X_1 + X_2 + |X_2| + 0.5\,X_1X_2 + a(1 + 0.5\,X_1)$, with standard normal noise, so the true average treatment effect is $\psi_0 = 1$. Both nuisance functions are fit by scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoosting` with 100 trees, either untuned (depth 3 and learning rate 0.1, the scaffold's placeholder) or regularized (depth 2 and learning rate 0.03). A third pair of fits uses main-terms linear and logistic regressions, which are misspecified for both functions. There are 1000 repetitions. Regularized boosting shows the emulation working, with every scheme agreeing within Monte Carlo error. Untuned boosting shows the two effects of a noisy learner described above. On the full sample, with no cross-fitting, untuned boosting undercovers badly, which is the overfitting that cross-fitting and the three-draw split both prevent. The main-terms linear fits are wrong for both nuisance functions, a case the formulas above do not cover. They did not separate the schemes on this process, and their bias was within Monte Carlo error of zero. So a wrong limit can separate the schemes, but need not.
